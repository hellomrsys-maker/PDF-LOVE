Where the size actually is

Text weighs almost nothing. A forty-page contract, all words, is typically well under a megabyte, because the file stores character codes and a font rather than a picture of every letter. Images are where the size lives. A single scanned page at 300 DPI in colour is several megabytes on its own, which is why a four-page scan can be larger than a two-hundred-page novel.

That one fact predicts almost everything about compression. If your file is big because it is a scan or full of photographs, it will shrink a lot. If it is big for some other reason — hundreds of pages of text, embedded fonts, a huge attachment — compression will barely move it, and the honest answer is a different approach.

What compression is trading away

Compressing a PDF means re-encoding the images inside it: fewer pixels, more aggressive JPEG quality, colour reduced where it adds nothing. Text is not touched, so it stays perfectly crisp no matter how far you push the setting. What degrades is photographic detail and, on scans, the sharpness of the letterforms — pushed too far, scanned text develops soft edges and a faint mottling around each character.

This is why the preview matters more than the number. Look at a page with the smallest text in the document, at 100% zoom, and decide whether it is still comfortable to read. A file at 3 MB you can read beats one at 1.5 MB you cannot.

When compression is not the answer

  • It is already small and still too big. A 60 MB text-only PDF is usually carrying embedded attachments or full font sets. Compression has nothing to work on. Split it instead.
  • You need the resolution. Anything going to a printer, a court filing, or an archive should not be compressed to fit an email. Send a link instead, or split it.
  • You are 10% over. Removing a couple of pages that do not need to be there is faster and lossless.

Splitting is underrated here. Attachment limits are per message, not per day, so two emails carrying 12 MB each will arrive when one 24 MB message bounces. It costs the recipient one extra click and costs you no quality at all.

The limits you are actually up against

Gmail and Outlook both refuse attachments over roughly 25 MB, and that figure is measured after encoding, which adds about a third — so a 20 MB file is nearer 27 MB on the wire and can bounce even though it looks like it should fit. Aim for 15 MB if you want it to arrive without thinking about it. Corporate mail servers frequently set lower limits than the consumer services, often 10 MB, and the bounce message may take hours to come back.

Checking the result

Open the compressed file, not just its size in the file listing. Check a page with photographs, a page with the smallest text, and any page with a signature or a stamp on it — those are where over-compression shows first. Then confirm the page count matches the original. Finally, keep the uncompressed version: compression is one-way, and the day someone asks for the print-quality original is the day you will want it.

Worth knowing: greyscale is a legitimate compression step for anything that was going to be read or printed in black and white anyway. Dropping the colour channels from a scanned document routinely halves it before you touch the quality setting at all.
