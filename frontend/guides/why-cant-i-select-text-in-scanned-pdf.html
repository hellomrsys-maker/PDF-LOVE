Why can't I select text in my scanned PDF?

A scanned document is really just a photograph saved as a PDF — there's no real text in it, only pixels. That's why you can't select, search, or copy anything from it. The fix is OCR (optical character recognition), which reads the image and adds an invisible, searchable text layer underneath.

Why a scan has no text in it

When a scanner or a phone camera captures a page, it records brightness values — a grid of pixels. It has no idea that some of those pixels form the word invoice. Wrapping that image in a PDF container changes the file extension and nothing else: you now have a photograph in a document wrapper. Every symptom follows from that. Ctrl-F finds nothing because there is nothing to find. You cannot copy a paragraph because there are no characters to copy. Converting the file to Word produces an empty document, which is the single most common "the converter is broken" report we see and is not a converter problem at all.

A PDF exported from Word, by contrast, contains real character codes and the font information needed to draw them. That is why one PDF behaves like a document and another behaves like a picture, even though both open in the same reader and look much the same on screen.

How to tell which kind you have, in five seconds

Try to select a line of text with your cursor. If a neat blue highlight follows the words, the text is real. If you instead get a rectangular selection box that ignores where the words are — or nothing happens at all — it is an image. A second check: zoom in to 400%. Real text stays crisp at any zoom because it is drawn from vector outlines; a scan turns into visible squares.

What OCR actually does

Optical character recognition looks at the shapes in the image, decides which characters they are, and writes those characters into the PDF as an invisible layer positioned exactly behind what you see. The page looks completely unchanged — the original scan is still what is displayed — but underneath it there is now selectable, searchable, copyable text. This is why a searchable PDF is sometimes called a "sandwich": image on top, text below.

It also means OCR is a recognition step, not a magic one. It is guessing at characters from pixels, and the quality of the guess depends almost entirely on the quality of the pixels.

What goes wrong, and how to tell

  • Garbled output. Words come out as 1nv0lce or rn where m should be. That is low resolution. OCR wants around 300 DPI; a phone photo held close and cropped is often effectively 150 and the engine has too few pixels per character to work with.
  • Whole lines missing. Usually skew. A page scanned a few degrees off straight breaks line detection, because the engine looks for horizontal rows of characters.
  • Only part of the page recognised. Uneven lighting. A shadow across one half drops the contrast below what the engine can threshold, and it treats that region as blank.
  • Wrong language entirely. Accented characters and non-Latin scripts need the right language model selected; running Devanagari through the English model produces confident nonsense.

In every one of those cases, rescanning is far more effective than running OCR again with different settings. Recognition cannot recover information the scan never captured. Put the page flat, square to the camera, on a contrasting surface, with light coming from the side rather than over your shoulder — and if your scanner offers a DPI setting, use 300.

Checking that it worked

Open the finished PDF and search for a word you can see near the middle of the page. If the reader jumps to it and highlights it, the text layer is there and aligned. Then select a whole paragraph and paste it into a text editor: that tells you the recognition quality, which searching alone does not. Expect a small number of errors on any real-world scan — OCR at 98% accuracy still means roughly one wrong character per two lines, which is perfectly usable for searching and needs a proofread before you rely on it as a quotation.

Worth knowing: the engine here is Tesseract, compiled to WebAssembly. It is the same open-source engine behind a great deal of desktop scanning software — the difference is only that it is running in your browser tab instead of on a server, so the document never leaves your machine.

Adobe Acrobat and cloud scanning apps offer this too, but usually as a paid feature or a cloud round-trip that takes your document off your device. PDF Love's OCR runs entirely on-device, so a private document (a contract, a medical form) never has to leave your computer to become searchable.
