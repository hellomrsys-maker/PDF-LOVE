Why GIFs get so large

GIF is a 1987 format that has outlived far better ones for a single reason: it plays anywhere, with no player, no controls and no autoplay policy to negotiate. Everything else about it is a limitation. It allows a palette of 256 colours per frame, and it has no interframe compression worth the name — where a modern video codec stores "this frame is the last one with these forty pixels changed", GIF largely stores each frame afresh.

That is why a 10 MB video clip becomes a 40 MB GIF at full size and frame rate, and why the answer is always subtraction. Three levers, in order of effect:

  • Length. Size scales linearly with duration. Three seconds instead of ten is a two-thirds saving before anything else.
  • Dimensions. Halving the width quarters the pixel count. 480 pixels wide is plenty for something viewed inline in a message or a document.
  • Frame rate. Video runs at 30 frames per second; GIFs read fine at 10 to 15. That alone is a halving.

Why the watermark is there in the first place

A cloud converter pays for CPU time and bandwidth on every clip it processes, so the free tier has to lead somewhere — hence the watermark, the clip-length cap and the daily limit. When the encoding happens in your own browser, each conversion costs the site nothing, which removes the reason for all three. The trade is that your device does the work: a long clip takes longer on a phone than it would on a server farm, and you can watch it happening.

What will look bad, and why

The 256-colour palette decides this. Content with few distinct colours survives beautifully — screen recordings, UI demonstrations, animation, line art. Content with smooth gradients does not: a sunset, a shallow-focus background or a subtle lighting change gets quantised into visible bands, because there simply are not enough palette entries to describe a gradient smoothly. Dithering hides banding by scattering pixels between two available colours, which looks better but compresses worse, so it costs size.

Film footage with grain is the worst case: the grain changes every pixel every frame, defeating what little interframe compression GIF has, and the file balloons.

Where it is going matters

Check the limit at the destination before you start. Many chat and forum platforms cap inline GIFs at a few megabytes and will silently refuse or re-encode anything larger — and their re-encode will look worse than the one you would have chosen. Slack, Discord, GitHub and most issue trackers all have their own ceiling, and the number is easy to look up in advance.

Checking the result

Play it through twice at the size it will actually be shown. Look for banding in any smooth areas, confirm the loop point does not jump distractingly, and check the start and end are where you meant them to be. If the file is too large, go back to length first — it is nearly always the cheapest cut and the least visible.

Worth knowing: GIF has no audio track at all. If the clip needs sound, a short MP4 is the right format — smaller, better looking, and supported everywhere GIFs are these days.
