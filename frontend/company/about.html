About Dockbench

104 tools for PDFs, images and video. Ninety-seven of them never send your file anywhere — they run inside your browser, on your own hardware.

The idea

Most online file tools work the same way: you upload your document to someone else's server, they process it, you download the result. That model has an obvious problem. Your contract, your passport scan, your medical record — it all sits on a machine you don't control, under a retention policy you have to take on faith.

Dockbench inverts that. The browser you're already running is a capable computer. PDF parsing, image processing, OCR, even AES-256 encryption all run perfectly well on it. So we compiled the real engines to WebAssembly and ship them with the app. Your file is read from disk, processed in memory, and written straight back to your downloads folder. No request is made because there is nothing to send.

You can verify this yourself

Open your browser's developer tools, switch to the Network tab, and use any on-device tool. No request fires. That's the whole claim, and it's checkable in about fifteen seconds — which is the point. Trust that can be verified isn't really trust, it's just arithmetic.

What that buys you

  • No daily limits. There's no per-task cost to us, so there's no meter. Merge two hundred PDFs if you want to.
  • No account. There's nothing to log into because there's no server-side state to attach to you.
  • Works offline. After the first visit the app is cached, including the OCR engine and the encryption engine. Airplane mode is fine.
  • Large files stay fast. Nothing is bottlenecked on your upload speed, because nothing is uploaded.

The honest exceptions

Seven of the 104 tools genuinely need a server, because they depend on engines too large to ship to a browser — LibreOffice for full-fidelity Office conversion, Ghostscript for deep compression, and a local LLM for chat-with-document. These tools only appear when a backend is actually reachable, and they're clearly labelled server-assisted in the interface. You can run that backend yourself; it's open-source and documented. The upcoming desktop app bundles it, so even those seven run on your own machine.

One more: the currency converter fetches live exchange rates. Rates come from somewhere by definition. No file or personal data is involved.

Open source

Every dependency in the stack is free and open-source. There is no paid vendor API anywhere in it. You can read the code, audit the privacy claims, self-host the backend, or fork the whole thing.
